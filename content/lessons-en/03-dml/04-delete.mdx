<TlDr>`DELETE` removes rows that match a condition. `DELETE ... USING` can use another table to find them; `TRUNCATE` clears a whole table quickly.</TlDr>

## What's in the data

`review` has ten reviews. Two have ratings below four: reviews 5 and 7. Books in category 6, Python, have two reviews.

## DELETE with WHERE

```sql run expect=rows:2
delete from review where rating < 4 returning review_id, rating;
```

On the board, `WHERE` selects the rows rated 3 and 2. They turn red, are crossed out and disappear; the other rows remain. `RETURNING` shows which rows were deleted.

The condition is checked before deletion. With `where review_id = 5`, one row is deleted regardless of its rating.

## DELETE ... USING

Sometimes the condition needs a column from another table. You can use `USING`:

```sql run expect=rows:2
delete from review r
using book b
where r.book_id = b.book_id and b.category_id = 6
returning r.review_id, b.title;
```

`USING` adds a table to the query, much like `FROM` in `SELECT`. On the board, Postgres reads `review` and `book`, joins them to find reviews of books in category 6, then deletes those reviews. A subquery or a separate lookup of book IDs could also supply the condition.

## Compare TRUNCATE

```sql run expect=rows:0
truncate review;
```

All ten rows fade at once. `DELETE` processes rows and checks the condition; `TRUNCATE` clears the whole table without scanning the rows. It is faster on large tables, but has no `WHERE` and takes a stronger lock.

`DELETE` reports the number of rows deleted. `TRUNCATE` does not return rows or process them one by one.

## What's missing and why

- ❌ The two reviews rated below 4 are absent after `DELETE`: they matched `WHERE`.
- ❌ Reviews of books in category 6 are absent after `DELETE ... USING`: the join found them.
- ❌ No reviews remain after `TRUNCATE`: it cleared the table.
- ❌ The database has none of these changes after preview: the deletions rolled back.

## Pitfalls

<Callout kind="trap">
`DELETE` without `WHERE` removes every row individually. Unlike `TRUNCATE`, it fires `ON DELETE` triggers and logs individual row deletions. Both commands can be rolled back in PostgreSQL, but a large `DELETE` can take time and generate a lot of log data. Check your condition with `SELECT` first:

```sql noexec
select review_id, rating from review where rating < 4;
```
</Callout>

<Callout kind="tip">
After a large `DELETE`, disk space is not immediately reclaimed. Once old row versions are no longer needed, ordinary `VACUUM` makes their space available for reuse, but usually does not return it to the operating system. `VACUUM FULL` can shrink the file, but needs extra space and blocks access to the table while it runs.
</Callout>

## Check yourself

2. Why does `DELETE ... USING` need a second table in this example?
3. How does `TRUNCATE review` differ from `DELETE FROM review`?

<Cheatsheet>
- `delete from t where condition;`
- `DELETE` reports the number of deleted rows.
- `DELETE ... USING` adds a table for the condition, like `FROM`.
- Without `WHERE`, every row in the table is deleted.
- `TRUNCATE` clears the whole table and has no `WHERE`.
- Both commands are transactional in PostgreSQL; `TRUNCATE` is faster and takes a stronger lock.
</Cheatsheet>
