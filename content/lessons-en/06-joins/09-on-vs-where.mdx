<TlDr>In a `LEFT JOIN`, `ON` determines which rows match. `WHERE` tests the joined row and can remove a left row the join preserved.</TlDr>

## What's in the data

There are 6 authors in <T id="public.author">author</T> and 11 books in <T id="public.book">book</T>. Three books cost more than 1000: Fluent Python at 2400 by Luciano Ramalho, Simple Python at 1990 and Learning Python at 3200. The last two have no author.

Of the six authors, only Ramalho has a book over the threshold. Boris Pasternak has no books at all.

## The condition in ON

Show all authors, with a book only if it costs more than 1000:

```sql run expect=rows:6
select a.name, b.title
from author a
left join book b
  on a.author_id = b.author_id and b.price > 1000
order by a.name;
```

Six rows, one per author. Ramalho has Fluent Python in `title`; the other five have NULL.

`ON` determines which pairs match. Both parts must be true: the author identifier matches, and the price exceeds 1000. If an author has no book satisfying both, `LEFT JOIN` keeps the author with NULL in the book columns.

The condition in `ON` does not remove left rows. It determines which right rows can be paired with them.

On the board, the join produces five NULL-filled rows and one row for Ramalho, six total.

## The same condition in WHERE

Move the price check to `WHERE`:

```sql run expect=rows:1
select a.name, b.title
from author a
left join book b on a.author_id = b.author_id
where b.price > 1000
order by a.name;
```

One row: Ramalho. The previous query returned six.

Logically, the join comes before `WHERE`. Without the price condition in `ON`, it gives 10 rows: 9 pairs and Pasternak with NULL book columns. `WHERE` then tests each row. Of the other nine rows, some have prices below 1000 and two have NULL prices: one is a book without a price and one is Pasternak's unmatched row. Comparisons with NULL yield `UNKNOWN`; `WHERE` keeps only `TRUE`.

The board shows 10 rows after the join and 1 of 10 after `WHERE`.

Here the right-side filter removes the unmatched row, leaving the same result as an inner join with the same filter.

## Find authors without an expensive book

The `ON` condition also helps answer the reverse question. Keep only rows with no match:

```sql run expect=rows:5
select a.name
from author a
left join book b
  on a.author_id = b.author_id and b.price > 1000
where b.book_id is null
order by a.name;
```

Five rows: every author except Ramalho. Pasternak has no books; the other four have no book over 1000. Their prices are below the threshold or missing.

`book_id` is the primary key, so a real book cannot have NULL there. Here NULL means no pair matched. In this particular query, `b.price is null` also identifies unmatched rows: a matched book must have a price over 1000. In a join without that price condition, a matched book can have a NULL price, so its primary key is the safer match test. Lesson 6.11 covers this anti-join pattern.

## What's missing and why

- ❌ The other five authors in the `WHERE` version: their matched books are not over 1000 or have no price, or they have no matching book.
- ❌ Cheaper books and the book without a price in the `ON` version: they are not matches under the price condition, so their authors appear with NULL book columns unless another book matches.
- ❌ Pasternak in the `WHERE` version: he has no books and the filter requires a price over 1000.
- ❌ Books by Ramalho and Verne in the query with `a.country = 'Russia'` in `ON`: those authors are not from Russia, so no pair matches. The authors still appear with NULL book columns.
- ❌ Other books in the expensive-book query: only Ramalho's one book meets `price > 1000` and has an author.

## Pitfalls

<Callout kind="trap">
A condition on the left table in `ON` does not filter left rows. It changes which pairs match:

```sql run expect=rows:9
select a.name, b.title
from author a
left join book b
  on a.author_id = b.author_id and a.country = 'Russia'
order by a.name, b.title;
```

Nine rows. Three Russian authors with books get all six of their books. Pasternak, Ramalho and Verne remain as one NULL-filled row each. Ramalho and Verne are not from Russia, so their books do not match, but the authors remain.

To show only Russian authors, put that filter in `WHERE` instead.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="tip">
Filtering authors in `WHERE`:

```sql run expect=rows:7
select a.name, b.title
from author a
left join book b on a.author_id = b.author_id
where a.country = 'Russia'
order by a.name, b.title;
```

Seven rows: six books by Russian authors and Pasternak with NULL for the book. The left-side condition filters the joined rows after the join.

Think of `ON` as “is this a match?” and `WHERE` as “should this joined row appear?”
</Callout>

2. How many authors remain if you add `where a.country = 'Russia'` to the first query? Check on the board.
3. Why is `b.book_id is null` a safer general match test than `b.price is null`? Do the two tests differ in this particular anti-join, and why?

<Cheatsheet>
- `ON` determines matches before unmatched left rows are added.
- `WHERE` filters the joined rows.
- A right-side `WHERE` condition that rejects NULL removes unmatched left rows.
- A left-side condition in `ON` does not remove left rows; failed matches get NULL on the right.
- Find no match using a non-nullable right key: `where right_table.pk is null`.
- This is the logical order; PostgreSQL may choose an equivalent execution plan.
</Cheatsheet>
