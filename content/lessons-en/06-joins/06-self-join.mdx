<TlDr>A `SELF JOIN` is an ordinary `JOIN` with the same table on both sides. Distinct names give its rows two roles, letting a query pair an employee with their manager.</TlDr>

## What's in the data

There are 8 people in <T id="public.employee">employee</T>. Each has an <C id="public.employee.employee_id">employee_id</C> and a <C id="public.employee.manager_id">manager_id</C> reference.

Olga Nikolaeva is the director; her manager_id is NULL. The other seven people reference someone. Alexey Morozov earns 190000, while his manager Pavel Orlov earns 180000. Tatiana Zaitseva is on the fourth level of the tree.

## Two instances of one table

To pair each employee with their manager, join the table to itself:

```sql run expect=rows:7
select e.name, m.name as boss
from employee e
join employee m on e.manager_id = m.employee_id
order by e.employee_id;
```

Seven rows. Pavel Orlov and Svetlana Volkova report to Olga; Dmitry and Alexey to Pavel; Natalia and Igor to Svetlana; Tatiana to Dmitry.

There are eight employees but seven rows. Olga has no match: her manager_id is `NULL`, and an ordinary equality comparison with NULL does not return `TRUE`. `INNER JOIN` excludes her row in the employee role.

On the board, employee is scanned twice: alias `e` on the left and `m` on the right. These are two roles for the same table. The join has 7 pairs. Olga has no match as an employee; Alexey, Natalia, Igor and Tatiana have no matches as managers because nobody reports to them.

## Why use aliases?

`e` and `m` are **table aliases**, like `b` and `a` in lesson 6.2. They do not rename the table in the schema. They provide distinct names inside the query so you can write `e.name` and `m.name`.

Without distinct names, Postgres cannot tell the instances apart:

```sql run expectError=42712
select * from employee join employee on employee.manager_id = employee.employee_id;
```

The error is `42712`: employee is specified twice. At least one instance needs an alias; giving both aliases makes their roles clear.

Another error: the aliases exist, but the column is not qualified.

```sql run expectError=42702
select name from employee e join employee m on e.manager_id = m.employee_id;
```

The error is `42702`: `name` exists in both instances. Write `e.name` or `m.name`.

## Who earns more than their manager?

The condition assigns the roles. Employee on the left and manager on the right gives `e.manager_id = m.employee_id`.

```sql run expect=rows:1
select e.name, e.salary, m.name as boss, m.salary as boss_salary
from employee e
join employee m on e.manager_id = m.employee_id
where e.salary > m.salary
order by e.employee_id;
```

One row: Alexey Morozov earns 190000; his manager Pavel Orlov earns 180000. The other employees earn less than their managers or have no manager.

`SELF JOIN` can answer hierarchy questions: who reports to whom, how many reports a manager has, and who earns more than their manager.

## A director without a manager

`INNER JOIN` omitted Olga. To keep all employees, including the director, use `LEFT JOIN`:

```sql run expect=rows:8
select e.name, m.name as boss
from employee e
left join employee m on e.manager_id = m.employee_id
order by e.employee_id;
```

Eight rows. Olga has NULL in `boss`; the other rows are unchanged.

## What's missing and why

- ❌ Olga Nikolaeva in the employee role in `INNER JOIN`: manager_id is NULL, so she has no match.
- ❌ Alexey, Natalia, Igor and Tatiana in the `boss` column: nobody references their identifiers as a manager.
- ❌ Other employees in the salary comparison: they do not earn more than their managers, or have none.
- ❌ Olga's manager in `LEFT JOIN`: she has none, so `boss` is NULL.

## Pitfalls

<Callout kind="trap">
Reverse the condition and the roles reverse too. The query runs, but its labels become misleading:

```sql run expect=rows:7
select e.name as employee, m.name as boss
from employee e
join employee m on e.employee_id = m.manager_id
order by m.employee_id;
```

Seven rows, with “employee Olga Nikolaeva, boss Pavel Orlov” first. Pavel actually reports to Olga. `e.employee_id = m.manager_id` makes `e` the manager and `m` the report, while the output labels say the opposite. Check which side holds manager_id.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="tip">
For an employee hierarchy, use a foreign key from manager_id to employee_id and prevent cycles in the application or with a trigger. A self join does not create cycles, but cyclic data can produce unexpected pairs and can make a recursive query repeat indefinitely unless it handles cycles.
</Callout>

2. Who manages nobody? Use a `left join` and keep rows without a matching report.
3. What changes if you swap the roles of `e` and `m` in the main condition but keep the column labels?

<Cheatsheet>
- `SELF JOIN` is an ordinary `JOIN` of a table to itself, not a separate SQL keyword.
- Give the table instances distinct names; `employee e` and `employee m` make both roles clear.
- `e.manager_id = m.employee_id`: e is the employee, m the manager.
- `INNER JOIN` excludes the root whose manager_id is NULL; `LEFT JOIN` keeps it with NULL for the manager.
- Duplicate table names give `42712`; an unqualified column present in both instances gives `42702`.
- The direction of the condition determines the roles. Check the output labels.
</Cheatsheet>
